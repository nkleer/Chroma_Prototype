# Science, Research & Discovery — expanded design v2

A modern-Earth individual-simulator expansion about investigating the world, creating trustworthy knowledge and choosing a sustainable scientific life. This is a proposed design specification, not a claim that the systems have been playtested. Occupational requirements must be supplied by the chosen country, institution and discipline.

## 1. Consistency corrections

| Earlier ambiguity | Revised rule |
|---|---|
| Laboratory, computational and field research looked like separate ranks. | These are methods and work settings. One scientist may combine all three within one career commitment. |
| A computational researcher could qualify through one useful contribution. | A contribution can earn a perk or achievement. A role requires sustained responsibility and recognition appropriate to its setting. |
| Independent researcher meant working outside employment. | Distinguish independent research judgment from unaffiliated employment status. An employed scientist can be intellectually independent; an unaffiliated investigator can still need mentorship. |
| Scientific careers seemed to lead toward group leadership. | Specialist, technical, public, applied and leadership routes have distinct milestones and legitimate destinations. |
| Publication looked like a measure of research quality. | Keep output, evidence quality, visibility, use and contribution attribution separate. |
| Colors appeared to imply characteristic weaknesses. | A color expression supplies priorities and possible tensions. Its weaknesses are contextual possibilities, never automatic penalties. |
| Formal authority followed directly from demonstrated competence. | Competence, authorization, appointment and available infrastructure are separate requirements. |
| Every opportunity could produce another title bonus. | One career commitment owns overlapping duties; method facets and achievements do not create duplicate obligations or bonuses. |

## 2. The scientific life being simulated

Track six distinct aspects of a scientific life without combining them into a universal success score:

| Aspect | What it captures | Examples of progress |
|---|---|---|
| Capability | What the person can actually do, within specified domains. | Design a controlled study; validate an analysis; maintain an instrument. |
| Access | Facilities, information, permissions and support available now. | Laboratory affiliation; open data; field partnership; instrument booking. |
| Contribution | The useful scientific work the person has produced or enabled. | Dataset, method, replication, model, synthesis, maintained infrastructure. |
| Recognition | Who knows or values that contribution. | Technical peers, a local community, employers, a scholarly field. |
| Agency | Influence over questions, methods, schedule and resource allocation. | Negotiate a project scope; lead a work package; choose an independent question. |
| Sustainability | Whether this working life can continue. | Income continuity, manageable workload, supportive relationships, feasible location. |

Recognition is audience-specific; agency is scope-specific. High public visibility does not grant laboratory authority. Low visibility does not erase contribution. Sustainability includes external working conditions rather than being solely an individual resilience trait. Use existing simulator resources for money, time, health and relationships; do not create duplicate currencies.

Satisfaction follows the individual's priorities. One person wants discovery, another useful applications, another craft mastery, another autonomy, and another a stable job that leaves room for family. Priorities can change.

## 3. Modular subject domains

Choose a primary subject domain and optional adjacent expertise. Specialization provides depth; adjacent expertise needs evidence before it grants capability.

| Domain module | Typical work | Distinctive constraints |
|---|---|---|
| Living Systems & Biotechnology | Cultivation, ecology, physiology, molecular work, bioprocesses. | Biological variability, contamination, growth times, living-material care. |
| Chemistry & Materials | Synthesis, characterization, formulation, material testing. | Instrument access, chemical handling, standards, batch variation. |
| Physics & Astronomy | Measurement, modeling, instrumentation, observation. | Detector limitations, observation windows, substantial shared infrastructure. |
| Earth, Climate & Environment | Monitoring, sampling, environmental modeling. | Seasons, spatial coverage, historical baselines, site access. |
| Computing, Mathematics & Data | Proofs, algorithms, simulations, scientific software. | Formal assumptions, compute access, data suitability, maintenance. |
| Health & Clinical Research | Clinical studies, public health, diagnostics research. | Participant protection, approvals, recruitment and clinical partnerships. |
| Social & Behavioral Research | Interviews, surveys, observation, experiments, archives. | Consent, context, sampling, interpretation, positionality. |
| History, Archaeology & Scholarly Inquiry | Archives, material evidence, interpretation, comparative scholarship. | Provenance, source access, preservation and contested interpretation. |

The last module is optional if the game uses “science” narrowly. The common research systems can support scholarship, but laboratory validation and statistical inference are not universal requirements. Astronomers cannot repeat cosmic events on demand; qualitative research does not become invalid because its evidence is not numerical. Clinical research is an interface with Healthcare, not permission to treat patients.

## 4. Role titles, methods and stages

Assign one primary career role to the current sustained commitment. Add method facets such as experimental, computational, observational, field, qualitative, archival or theoretical. Add responsibility facets such as mentoring or work-package coordination. Facets describe scope without stacking titles.

| Role title | Plausible entry | Core responsibility | Credible destinations |
|---|---|---|---|
| Research Assistant | Relevant preparation and supervised appointment. | Deliver assigned research tasks reliably. | Specialist work, further study, coordination, adjacent employment. |
| Research Technician | Technical training, apprenticeship or equivalent demonstrated experience appropriate to the employer. | Operate and sustain procedures, equipment and materials. | Senior technical specialist, facility lead, method developer. |
| Research Scientist | Sustained responsibility for scientific investigation and appropriate preparation. | Develop questions, interpret evidence and contribute defensible findings. | Specialist, applied scientist, project lead, independent investigator. |
| Research Software Engineer | Demonstrated software engineering applied to research and a sustained appointment or equivalent commitment. | Build reliable software and workflows used by researchers. | Technical leadership, infrastructure development, specialist consulting. |
| Research Data Steward | Demonstrated data-management expertise in the relevant setting. | Maintain usable, documented and appropriately accessible research data. | Infrastructure leadership, governance, specialist practice. |
| Research Project Lead | Assigned responsibility for a defined project. | Coordinate scientific work, people, resources and delivery. | Further project leadership, scientific specialization, group leadership. |
| Research Group Leader | Institutional appointment or equivalent responsibility for a group. | Establish direction, support people and sustain the research program. | Continued leadership, institutional roles, return to specialist work. |
| Research Facility Lead | Responsibility for a shared technical service and the necessary technical competence. | Maintain capability, access, training and service continuity. | Larger facilities, infrastructure strategy, independent specialist work. |
| Evidence Synthesis Specialist | Demonstrated competence in appropriate review and synthesis methods. | Compare evidence transparently and explain confidence and gaps. | Policy interfaces, research methods, applied consultancy. |
| Participatory Research Coordinator | Sustained responsibility for a collaborative research initiative. | Coordinate participants, methods, feedback and shared expectations. | Community research, institutional partnership, public engagement. |
| Independent Investigator | Sustained, accountable inquiry with the resources and permissions required. | Develop and execute a self-directed research agenda. | Affiliation, specialist practice, collaboration, continued independent work. |
| Science Communication Specialist | Sustained responsibility for accurate audience-appropriate communication. | Translate evidence and uncertainty into usable public understanding. | Museums, journalism interfaces, public engagement leadership. |

Actual job labels vary. Map local labels to responsibilities. A postdoctoral appointment is a career/employment stage, not a universal competence certificate. A professor appointment is an institutional title with locally defined teaching, research and service duties. Preserve these facts as colorless appointment metadata attached to the current career commitment.

Community contributors can participate as Citizen Scientist, Community Observer or Volunteer Research Organizer in a community slot. If that same work becomes their career, move the commitment into the career slot rather than counting it twice.

Use supervised, independently executing, independently directing and enabling others as competence descriptions for specific work. Independence in one method does not imply independence in another.

## 5. Entry routes and constraints

| Entry route | Initial access | Meaningful limitations |
|---|---|---|
| Degree route | Structured study, supervisors, institutional facilities. | Admission, financing, time and local qualification requirements. |
| Technical apprenticeship | Hands-on practice and supervised technical responsibility. | Employer availability, authorization and exposure to broader theory. |
| Professional transition | Existing engineering, healthcare, farming or other domain experience. | Research-method gaps and protected professional boundaries. |
| Public participation | Open observations, community projects, selected public datasets. | Data quality support and restricted facilities or activities. |
| Open computational inquiry | Open publications, datasets, tools and possible remote collaborators. | Compute cost, data suitability, specialized expertise and validation. |
| Independent scholarship | Archives, public collections, local knowledge or privately sustained study. | Source access, financial support and peer engagement. |
| Return after a break | Retained expertise, prior relationships and documented work. | Changed methods, expired authorization and access that must be renewed. |

A nondegree route can be powerful in suitable domains; it cannot automatically bypass a regulated role's requirements. Affiliation is neither a universal requirement nor a universal guarantee of good work. Money purchases some access, not expertise, trustworthy conclusions or consent.

## 6. Project lifecycle and varied outcomes

Each project has a question, evidence target, methods, resource plan, obligations, contributors and a review point. These are planning items, not additional resource meters.

1. Frame the question and investigate existing evidence.
2. Check access, feasibility and applicable permissions.
3. Design the inquiry and identify what would change the conclusion.
4. Collect or construct evidence with appropriate records.
5. Evaluate evidence, assumptions, limitations and alternative explanations.
6. Share, apply, preserve, revise or close the work.

Projects can loop back, pause or stop. The appropriate validation depends on the discipline: replication, triangulation, benchmark comparisons, independent implementation, additional observations, source criticism or expert examination.

| Outcome | What it means | Potential value |
|---|---|---|
| Supported finding | Evidence supports a bounded claim. | New understanding or a practical decision. |
| Informative null/negative result | An appropriate study did not support the proposed effect or explanation. | Narrowed possibilities and reduced repetition of unpromising work. |
| Inconclusive result | Evidence cannot distinguish the relevant explanations. | Documented limitations and a better next design; not an automatic contribution award. |
| Method or infrastructure contribution | A validated tool, procedure or maintained service enables work. | Improved reliability and capability for other people. |
| Useful dataset or synthesis | Well-described evidence becomes available within lawful and ethical limits. | Reuse, comparison and better decisions. |
| Failed execution | Missing or compromised evidence prevents the intended inference. | Learning is possible if causes are understood; a failure alone earns nothing. |
| Responsible closure | Continuing is unjustified or infeasible and obligations are handled. | Preserved resources, usable records and a defensible transition. |

Do not roll directly for “discovery.” Resolve feasibility, execution, evidence quality and interpretation separately using the existing simulation engine. Preparation influences controllable errors; uncertainty, access and external conditions still matter. Leadership cannot compensate for absent measurement expertise. A team can meet complementary requirements that no individual meets alone.

## 7. Perks: specific capability with bounded effects

Every perk records its scope, demonstration and currency: current, needs refresh or dormant. A dormant capability remains in history. Safety authorization can expire independently of skill. One repeated action does not establish mastery; demonstration should show reliable performance and understanding across relevant situations.

| Perk | Evidence needed | Usable effect | Boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Procedure Reliability | Appropriate execution with understood checks. | Independently perform the validated procedure when authorized. | Does not transfer to unrelated instruments or methods. |
| Contamination Diagnosis | Identify a source and verify corrective action. | Diagnose similar contamination patterns. | Organism and facility differences still matter. |
| Instrument Troubleshooting | Resolve documented faults within permitted scope. | Distinguish maintenance problems from measurement problems. | Restricted repairs require qualified service. |
| Experimental Design | Defensible controls, comparisons and sampling rationale. | Build or assess a design within known methods. | Resources and ethical limits remain. |
| Statistical Judgment | Justified analysis with assumptions and limitations. | Select and interpret suitable analyses. | Specialized methods may require consultation. |
| Qualitative Interpretation | Transparent analysis grounded in appropriate evidence. | Develop defensible contextual interpretations. | Does not convert impressions into universal findings. |
| Model Critique | Test assumptions, sensitivity and comparison with evidence. | Identify conditions under which a model is informative. | A model's precision is not proof of validity. |
| Reproducible Workflow | Another competent person reconstructs the analysis. | Update and transfer documented work. | Confidential data can require controlled reproduction. |
| Field Adaptation | Handle changing conditions without losing inferential purpose. | Adjust a field plan and document implications. | Convenient samples may alter what can be concluded. |
| Source Criticism | Evaluate provenance, context and reliability. | Assess documentary or historical evidence. | Missing evidence cannot be replaced by confidence. |
| Method Transfer | Validate a method in a new setting. | Adapt that method to comparable contexts. | A new setting requires renewed evaluation. |
| Evidence Synthesis | Transparent search, selection and critical comparison. | Integrate studies and explain disagreements. | Weak evidence remains weak after aggregation. |
| Research Mentorship | A learner gains verified independence with appropriate support. | Structure feedback and developmental tasks. | Teaching skill does not confer formal supervisory authority. |
| Credit Negotiation | Clarify contributions and resolve an actual ambiguity. | Establish workable contribution agreements. | No guarantee against later dispute. |
| Research Partnership | Deliver reciprocal work with clear expectations. | Develop collaborations across institutional boundaries. | Trust is relationship-specific. |
| Community Listening | Show how participant input changed relevant project decisions. | Improve questions, participation and feedback. | Engagement does not replace required consent. |
| Grant Development | Coherent scope, methods, budget and delivery plan. | Produce competitive proposals where eligible. | Awards depend on external selection and fit. |
| Project Triage | Redirect or stop work using explicit evidence and obligations. | Recognize sunk costs and preserve useful outputs. | Cannot erase contractual responsibilities. |
| Scientific Explanation | Accurate communication evaluated with the intended audience. | Explain findings and limits clearly. | Popularity does not establish accuracy. |
| Sustainable Handover | Another person continues the work using adequate documentation and support. | Enable continuity during absence or departure. | Requires time and receiving capacity. |
| Research Integrity Practice | Consistent transparent handling of data, claims and contributions. | Recognize and address relevant integrity concerns. | Does not grant immunity from error or institutional review. |
| Responsible Automation | Validate an automated workflow and inspect its failure modes. | Delegate appropriate tasks with traceability and review. | Automation cannot replace accountability or protected-data rules. |

## 8. Colors: diversification without occupational stereotypes

Only role expressions use colors. Base perks, credentials, project outcomes and statuses are colorless. Color variants inherit the same role requirements and opportunities; they do not produce duplicate bonuses.

| Expression | Coherent scientific motivation | Possible style of action |
|---|---|---|
| White | Dependable shared knowledge. | Maintain standards and address public needs. |
| Blue | Understanding and improvement. | Refine explanations and compare approaches. |
| Black | Agency and strategic independence. | Negotiate ownership, scope and resources. |
| Red | Passion and freedom of inquiry. | Pursue personally compelling questions and expressive collaboration. |
| Green | Context, continuity and belonging. | Observe systems in place and preserve accumulated understanding. |
| White–Blue | Evidence-supported shared decisions. | Develop methods institutions can trust and use. |
| White–Black | Durable public capacity through strategic influence. | Negotiate resources for neglected but valuable work. |
| White–Red | A felt duty to investigate consequential problems. | Combine public commitment with energetic inquiry. |
| White–Green | Stewardship of collective knowledge and places. | Maintain long-term observation networks and community archives. |
| Blue–Black | Expertise as a source of autonomy. | Build a scarce specialization and negotiate research independence. |
| Blue–Red | Curiosity combined with experimental invention. | Explore unconventional designs and test them carefully. |
| Blue–Green | Understanding through contextual adaptation. | Combine models with close observation of living or evolving systems. |
| Black–Red | Self-directed work with personal creative ownership. | Develop an independent agenda and negotiate unconventional partnerships. |
| Black–Green | Practical independence through stewardship. | Sustain a specialist practice and protect irreplaceable research resources. |
| Red–Green | Attachment and direct engagement. | Investigate a place, organism or tradition through sustained personal involvement. |
| White–Blue–Red | Rigorous inquiry with a public mission and creative energy. | Build engaging collaborative investigations around urgent questions. |
| White–Blue–Black | Institution-building through standards, expertise and negotiation. | Establish a capable research organization. |
| White–Blue–Green | Reliable knowledge built through contextual care. | Sustain careful long-term research and mentorship. |
| White–Black–Red | Public purpose pursued through influence and mobilization. | Build support for an ambitious research initiative. |
| White–Black–Green | Collective continuity supported by strategic stewardship. | Secure a facility or archive across funding transitions. |
| White–Red–Green | Shared belonging expressed through passionate inquiry. | Co-develop questions with a place-based community. |
| Blue–Black–Red | Independent expertise and inventive experimentation. | Build a technically credible unconventional research practice. |
| Blue–Black–Green | Strategic capability grounded in adaptive understanding. | Develop resilient methods and long-term expertise. |
| Blue–Red–Green | Exploratory understanding through invention and observation. | Combine field intuition, models and unusual experiments. |
| Black–Red–Green | A self-directed scientific life grounded in attachment. | Sustain independent inquiry around personally meaningful systems or places. |

These expressions can occur in any suitable subject. A Green mathematician might value structural relationships and intellectual continuity. A Red statistician can love exploratory questions while respecting inferential limits. A Black community scientist can pursue local bargaining power through evidence. A mono-Blue collaborator can be inventive through learning and iterative problem-solving. None requires a new title or a color bonus.

Colors may shift between commitments and over time. Evaluate the actual motive and approach rather than assigning colors from a job name, public visibility or personality adjective.

## 9. Institutions and relationships

An institution supplies opportunities and constraints, not a fixed moral identity.

| Setting | Opportunities | Recurring tensions |
|---|---|---|
| University | Teaching, research networks, broad methods. | Competing duties, grant dependency, supervision load. |
| Public research institute | Mission continuity and specialist infrastructure. | Mandate limits, public scrutiny, administrative priorities. |
| Industrial R&D | Applied problems, engineering support, development pathways. | Commercial timelines, confidentiality, product priorities. |
| Shared facility | Technical depth and collaboration across groups. | Service workload, maintenance funding, contribution recognition. |
| Community initiative | Local knowledge and meaningful participation. | Volunteer capacity, trust, continuity and ownership. |
| Independent practice | Scope autonomy and flexible partnerships. | Income, access, peer engagement and administrative workload. |
| Museum, archive or observatory | Collections and long-term records. | Preservation, access priorities and operational capacity. |

Track relationship-specific trust, reciprocity and unresolved obligations through the simulator's existing relationship system. A supportive mentor may have no funding. A rival may be an excellent reviewer. A technician may know more about a method than a senior scientist. Participants contribute agency and perspectives; they are not merely a recruitment resource.

Cooperation creates coordination costs as well as complementary capability. Authorship, employment hierarchy and actual contribution remain separate. Some technical contributions warrant authorship under applicable criteria; others warrant different explicit recognition. Do not automatically turn every service into authorship.

## 10. Events with causes and delayed consequences

Trigger events from the project's state and relationships. Avoid repetitive tragedy as the primary source of difficulty.

| Event and trigger | Plausible choices | Consequences to resolve |
|---|---|---|
| Method fails after material or conditions change. | Validate the change, seek specialist help, redesign or pause. | Resources, interpretable evidence, method learning. |
| Careful result contradicts the hypothesis. | Reassess explanation, replicate where suitable, share bounded conclusions. | Knowledge changes; recognition may lag. |
| An overlooked technical contribution becomes essential. | Revisit credit and responsibilities. | Trust, future collaboration, fair recognition. |
| Reviewer criticism reveals a real weakness. | Revise, collect justified evidence or narrow the claim. | Stronger work with additional time costs. |
| A review appears mistaken. | Explain with evidence, revise clarity or pursue an appropriate appeal. | Outcome depends on judgment and process, not assertiveness alone. |
| A project wins funding. | Accept a feasible scope, negotiate commitments or decline overload. | Opportunity, staffing, new obligations. |
| A grant is rejected despite a credible proposal. | Seek feedback, revise fit, pursue another route or stop. | External scarcity and fit remain distinct from competence. |
| A team member develops expertise beyond the leader's. | Delegate authority, build partnership, revise roles. | Capacity growth and an identity transition. |
| A community proposes a better question. | Co-design, negotiate boundaries or explain constraints. | Relevance, participation, scope changes. |
| A planned observation window is lost. | Extend timing, use alternative evidence or accept a narrower project. | Some opportunities are irrecoverable. |
| A collaboration succeeds unusually well. | Continue, formalize responsibilities or preserve a limited partnership. | More work is possible, but capacity still limits expansion. |
| Family circumstances require relocation. | Negotiate remote tasks, transfer projects, change sectors or pause. | Local access changes while expertise remains. |
| An archived dataset gains relevance. | Check documentation and reuse permissions; reassess evidence. | Prior stewardship enables a new opportunity. |
| An honest published error is found. | Investigate, inform collaborators and pursue an appropriate correction. | Knowledge repair; trust depends on circumstances and response. |
| Pressure to overstate a finding emerges. | Retain bounded claims, negotiate expectations or use appropriate reporting channels. | Short-term opportunity can conflict with credible work. |
| A long-term specialist receives a stable appointment. | Accept, negotiate duties or pursue another setting. | Stability can enable deep work without leadership promotion. |
| A junior colleague becomes independent. | Celebrate, create a new relationship or recruit and train a successor. | Successful mentorship can reduce immediate team capacity. |
| The person prefers craft to management. | Rebalance duties or move into a specialist role. | Less formal authority can coexist with greater satisfaction. |

Immediate outcomes do not determine the entire event. A shared dataset may be used years later; a rushed promise may create obligations months later. Schedule follow-up only where a concrete dependency exists, not as an arbitrary penalty.

| The independent analyst | Domain learning → public-data projects → validated workflows → research partnerships. | Self-directed specialization; no automatic facility access. |
| The community investigator | Local observations → supported study → participatory coordination → institutional partnership. | Useful evidence and shared local agency. |
| The academic investigator | Study → supervised research → scientist → independent agenda. | Discovery and mentorship, with leadership optional. |
| The applied translator | Industry experience → research methods → collaborative trials → implementation. | A useful improvement demonstrated under operating conditions. |
| The reluctant leader | Strong research → group responsibility → overload → specialist return. | A role closer to the work they value. |
| The returning researcher | Career break → method refresh → access renewal → adjusted project. | Continuity without pretending conditions stayed unchanged. |
| The late entrant | Established nonresearch career → adjacent inquiry → collaboration → sustained contribution. | Credible expertise that incorporates prior experience. |
| The evidence custodian | Data support → stewardship → reusable collection → major enabling contribution. | Lasting utility even with limited public visibility. |
| The researcher who leaves | Research practice → transferable skill recognition → policy, software, education or another field. | A legitimate next life, with prior achievements preserved. |

## 12. Worked example: Green–Red researcher, unexpected but credible

Mira works in freshwater ecology because a lake near her community matters deeply to her. Green expresses attachment and contextual understanding; Red expresses personal commitment and freedom of inquiry. Her role is Research Scientist with field and analytical facets. She is not automatically an activist, anti-technology or undisciplined.

She observes an unusual seasonal pattern. Field Awareness helps her distinguish sampling limitations from a plausible ecological change. She lacks specialist modeling expertise, so she collaborates with a computational colleague. Their capabilities complement each other; neither receives the other's perk.

The first analysis is inconclusive. They improve sampling rather than treating passion as evidence. A technician identifies an instrument drift, receives appropriate contribution recognition and helps validate the corrected measurements. Reliable evidence eventually supports a narrower claim than Mira hoped.

Local residents want a simple answer. Mira explains uncertainty and listens to their questions. This creates a public engagement opportunity, not an automatic Science Communicator career title. If sustained public work later becomes her primary job, her career commitment can change.

The project contributes a documented dataset and a limited finding. Mira gains method experience and stronger collaboration; public recognition depends on subsequent communication and use. Her attachment supplies persistence and relevance, but can also make disappointing evidence emotionally difficult. That tension arises from the situation, not a mandatory Green–Red debuff.

## 14. Qualitative consistency checks

These are design reviews, not simulation test results. Numerical balance remains unverified.

| Check | Expected handling |
|---|---|
| Wealthy novice purchases equipment. | Access increases; competence, permissions and valid inference do not appear automatically. |
| Brilliant scientist lacks facilities. | Can pursue feasible theoretical/open-data work or partnerships; cannot perform inaccessible experiments. |
| Technician has no doctorate. | Technical mastery and facility leadership remain possible where local requirements allow. |
| Doctorate holder enters a new discipline. | Transferable skills remain; domain-specific competence needs demonstration. |
| A project produces no positive result. | Assess whether it generated informative evidence; no automatic failure or reward. |
| One paper has several contributors. | Record each person's actual contributions without duplicating the same benefit. |
| One person combines field, laboratory and computational work. | One career role with several method facets. |
| A break ends employment and laboratory access. | History and capability persist; access and currency are reassessed. |
| A researcher gains public fame. | Visibility increases; accuracy and scientific authority require separate evidence. |
| A Black or Red researcher acts rigorously. | Entirely valid; integrity is independent of color. |
| A White or Blue researcher behaves irresponsibly. | Resolve behavior and consequences without color-based immunity. |
| A person leaves academia. | Preserve contributions and support transitions; no universal prestige-loss penalty. |
| A strong person faces poor working conditions. | Structural constraints affect feasibility; avoid attributing every setback to personal weakness. |
| A team maintains an existing method for years. | Recognize reliability and enabling contribution without requiring constant novelty. |

Before numerical implementation, playtest whether every route has affordable entry points, meaningful decisions and attainable satisfying destinations. Measure whether specialist and community routes receive useful opportunities, whether paperwork overwhelms inquiry, whether caregiving creates permanent exclusion, and whether visibility becomes a hidden universal victory score. Calibrate durations and costs by domain and local context rather than inventing universal promotion thresholds.

## 15. Research grounding and limits

The design separates real-world grounding from proposed game rules. It does not encode current eligibility rules from any specific funding call.

- The European research-career framework distinguishes supervised, recognized, established and leading research profiles, and recognizes careers across sectors. This supports separating competence/independence from local job names. Source: https://euraxess.ec.europa.eu/career-development/for-organisations/glossary-terms
- UKRI describes digital research technical professional development and flexible career pathways. This supports giving technical and infrastructure careers their own progression. Source: https://www.ukri.org/opportunity/ukri-digital-research-technical-professional-skills-networkplus/
- ALLEA's European Code of Conduct applies across disciplines and research settings and emphasizes research culture, data practices and stakeholder responsibilities. This supports making integrity common to all colors and institutional settings. Source: https://allea.org/code-of-conduct/

The role catalogue, perk effects, project resolution structure, color expressions and life arcs are simulator design proposals. Funding rates, salary differences, qualification gates and institutional practices need setting-specific data if implemented quantitatively.
