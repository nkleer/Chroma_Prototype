# Life events and everyday moments: a source file for Chroma

Research file, 2026-10-04. Built for the Chroma life simulator (one life from child to elder, in three worlds: modern
Earth first, a forager band about 40,000 years ago, a Tolkien-like magic world). Every section cites its sources
(URL or reference). Text is paraphrase; short quotes are marked. Rates marked **[data]** come from a cited study;
rates marked **[est]** are reasoned estimates from nearby data and say what they lean on.

Stages used throughout: child (about 4-11), juv (juvenile, about 12-17), ya (young adult, 18-29), adult (30-44),
mature (45-64), elder (65+).

World notes: **T** = tribal forager band (~40,000 years ago), **M** = Tolkien-like magic world (pre-modern village or
town base; magic only after a gift awakens). "none" means the moment does not exist there.

Response sets: each moment and event lists 5+ distinct ways a person could respond, spanning cautious, curious,
selfish, passionate and traditional answers (plus kind, defiant, playful or withdrawn ones where they fit). These are
plain acts, not color assignments; the library thread maps them to colors.

Contents (sections are appended as research proceeds):
- Part A. Sources and base data (life event scales, trauma surveys, base rates)
- Part B. Developmental tasks and rites of passage per stage, and stage-unlocking events
- Part C. Everyday moments (150+), by stage
- Part D. Big life events (80+), with yearly rates per stage, drivers and responses
- Part E. Outside drivers (25+ per source type)
- Part F. Wrongs and their everyday frequencies
- Part G. World notes: forager band and pre-modern village life; human universals

## Part A. Sources and base data

### A1. Life event scales (what the instruments count)

**Holmes & Rahe Social Readjustment Rating Scale (SRRS, 1967).** 43 adult events weighted in "life change units"
(LCU), from death of a spouse (100), divorce (73), separation (65), jail (63), death of close kin (63), own injury or
illness (53), marriage (50), being fired (47), reconciliation and retirement (45 each), down to vacation (13), a major
holiday (12) and a minor violation of the law (11). The scale mixes good and bad events (marriage, a new family member,
an outstanding personal achievement at 28, a vacation) and counts all change as strain. A youth version (38 items) adds
peer acceptance (67), failing a grade (56), not making a team or club (55), beginning to date (51), being suspended
(50), getting into drugs or alcohol (50), birth of a sibling (50), getting into the college of choice (43), and a
sibling leaving home (37). Totals: 300+ in a year = high illness risk, 150-299 = moderate. Main critique (Mechanic
1975): it does not separate welcome from unwelcome change, and items are vague.
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holmes_and_Rahe_Stress_Scale ; Holmes T.H. & Rahe R.H. (1967) J Psychosom Res 11:213-218.

Chroma use: the LCU weights give a ready **relative impact** order for big events (death of spouse > divorce >
separation > jail = close bereavement > own illness > marriage > fired...). Positive events carry real weight too.

**Life Experiences Survey (Sarason, Johnson & Siegel 1978).** 47 general events plus 10 for students; each event
the person had is rated from -3 (very negative) to +3 (very positive). Main lesson for Chroma: the same event (a move,
a pregnancy, a new job, retirement) is read as good by one person and bad by another, and the reading, not the event,
predicts strain. This matches the spec rule that a person reads outside events through their own filter.
Source: https://elcentro.sonhs.miami.edu/research/measures-library/les/index.html ; Sarason, Johnson & Siegel (1978)
J Consult Clin Psychol 46:932-946.

**Life Events Checklist for DSM-5 (LEC-5, Weathers et al. 2013).** 17 potentially traumatic event types: natural
disaster; fire or explosion; transport accident; serious accident at work, home or play; toxic exposure; physical
assault; assault with a weapon; sexual assault; other unwanted sexual experience; combat or war zone; captivity;
life-threatening illness or injury; severe human suffering; sudden violent death; sudden unexpected death of someone
close; serious injury or death you caused to someone else; any other very stressful event. Each can be "happened to
me / witnessed / learned about / part of my job". (For Chroma, sexual items stay off the act list and appear only as
past events in general words, per the spec.)
Source: https://www.thenationalcouncil.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/04/life-event-checklist-lec.pdf ;
https://www.policingtrauma.sociology.cam.ac.uk/files/weathers_et_al_2013_lec5.pdf

**Positive event instruments.** The Pleasant Events Schedule (MacPhillamy & Lewinsohn 1982; 320 small pleasant
activities such as laughing, being with happy people, being complimented, sleeping well, being outdoors) and the uplifts
half of the Hassles and Uplifts Scales (below) are the main lists of everyday joys. The Life Experiences Survey's +3
side covers big positive events (marriage, a new job, an outstanding achievement, a reconciliation, a birth).
Reference: MacPhillamy D.J. & Lewinsohn P.M. (1982) J Consult Clin Psychol 50:363-380.

### A2. Daily hassles and uplifts (Kanner, Coyne, Schaefer & Lazarus 1981)

Hassles (117 items) are the irritating, frustrating demands of ordinary days; uplifts (135 items) are the small
pleasures. Hassles predicted psychological symptoms better than major life events did. Most frequent uplifts in the
original middle-aged sample: relating well with your spouse or lover (76%), relating well with friends (74%),
completing a task (73%), feeling healthy (73%), getting enough sleep (70%), eating out (68%), meeting your
responsibilities (68%), visiting, phoning or writing someone (68%), spending time with family (67%), home being
pleasing to you (66%). Most frequent hassles in the middle-aged sample: concerns about weight, health of a family
member, rising prices, home maintenance, too many things to do, misplacing or losing things, yard work, property or
taxes, crime, physical appearance. In a student sample the top hassles were: not enough time (86%), too many things
to do (82%), troubling thoughts about the future (79%), too many interruptions (75%), misplacing things (73%), health of
a family member (66%), social obligations (64%), concerns about standards (62%), getting ahead (62%), too many
responsibilities (62%).
Sources: Kanner A.D. et al. (1981) J Behav Med 4:1-39; https://en.wikiversity.org/wiki/Motivation_and_emotion/Book/2013/Daily_hassles_and_uplifts ;
https://media.lanecc.edu/users/kime/Psy203stressA.pdf (student-sample percentages).

Chroma use: the uplifts list is dominated by **relationships going well** and **finishing things**; the hassles list by
**time pressure, money, upkeep, health worries and lost things**. Both kinds come up every week; uplifts are reported
about as often as hassles, which supports the spec's balanced tone.

### A3. Traumatic events worldwide (WHO World Mental Health surveys)

Benjet et al. (2016): 68,894 adults in 24 countries. 70.4% had at least one lifetime traumatic event (28.6% to 84.6%
by country); 30.5% had four or more; those exposed averaged 4.6 events. Lifetime prevalence by type: unexpected death
of a loved one 31.4%; witnessed death or serious injury 23.7%; mugged or threatened with a weapon 14.5%;
life-threatening car accident 14.0%; witnessed fights at home as a child 12.9%; life-threatening illness or injury
11.8%; beaten by a caregiver as a child 7.9%; child with a serious illness 7.9%; natural disaster 7.7%; civilian in a
war zone 5.0%; beaten by a partner 4.5%; man-made disaster 4.0%; combat 3.2%; sexual violence (kept as background
only) about 6%. Five types (witnessing death or injury, unexpected death of a loved one, being mugged, a life-threatening
car accident, a life-threatening illness or injury) made up over half of all exposures.
Drivers: **being married was the most consistent protective factor** (odds about 0.5-0.9); younger adults (18-34) had
higher odds of interpersonal, accident and injury events; men had more violence and injury events, women more
partner violence; lower education raised violence and accident risk; **prior interpersonal violence was the strongest
predictor of later trauma** (trouble clusters).
Sources: https://sdlab.fas.harvard.edu/resource/pdf-167 (full text); https://sdlab.fas.harvard.edu/publications/epidemiology-traumatic-event-exposure-worldwide-results-world-mental-health ;
Benjet C. et al. (2016) Psychol Med 46:327-343. Kessler R.C. et al. (2017) Trauma and PTSD in the WHO World Mental Health
Surveys, Eur J Psychotraumatol 8(sup5):1353383 (same 29-type list; the full text was blocked by a captcha, so figures
here come from Benjet 2016).

Chroma use: across a lifetime **most people (about 7 in 10) meet at least one very hard event**, and the commonest by far
is the sudden death of someone close. Per year this is roughly 1-3% for each of the common types (lifetime prevalence
spread over about 50 adult years, front-loaded in youth for accidents and violence, back-loaded for illness and loss).

### A5. How lifetime events cluster by stage (summary of the above)

- **child**: almost no own-body events; events reach the child through family (a sibling born, a move, a parent's job
  loss, a divorce, a grandparent's death). About 1% a year lose a parent or sibling.
- **juv**: peer events dominate (acceptance, first dating, team selection, school failure, first drink); violent
  victimisation already at adult levels (2% a year); depression peaks (18% a year).
- **ya**: peak of change: moves, jobs, first love and breakups, marriage, first child, accidents, crime victimisation
  (4.4% a year at 18-24), substance problems (27% SUD at 18-25).
- **adult**: births, career moves, promotions, mortgages, divorce peak in the first decade of marriage, first parent's
  death, the "sandwich" years.
- **mature**: parents' deaths, children leaving, first own serious illness (cancer above 0.5-1% a year), inheritance
  peaks near 60, gray divorce, job loss is harder to recover from.
- **elder**: retirement, widowhood, falls (more than 1 in 4 a year), hospital stays (17% a year), dementia, deaths of
  friends, grandchildren, own death (2-16% a year).

## Part B. Developmental tasks and rites of passage per stage

### B1. Frameworks used

- **Havighurst's developmental tasks (1948/1972).** Each stage brings tasks that arise from the body (learning to walk,
  puberty, menopause), from the person's own values (choosing work, a philosophy of life) and from society (learning
  to read, being a responsible citizen). Doing a task well makes later tasks easier; failing it brings disapproval and
  trouble later. Each task has a "teachable moment" when the body, the person and society are all ready.
  Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_J._Havighurst ;
  https://content.patnawomenscollege.in/home-sci/Developmental%20Tasks-%20Adulthood,%20middle%20age%20and%20old%20age.pdf ;
  Havighurst R.J. (1972) Developmental Tasks and Education, 3rd ed.
- **Erikson's psychosocial stages (1950, 1982).** Each stage poses a tension whose good resolution yields a strength:
  initiative vs guilt (preschool, purpose); industry vs inferiority (school age, competence); identity vs role
  confusion (adolescence, fidelity); intimacy vs isolation (young adulthood, love); generativity vs stagnation (middle
  adulthood, care); integrity vs despair (old age, wisdom). Reference: Erikson E.H. (1950) Childhood and Society;
  (1982) The Life Cycle Completed.
- **Arnett's emerging adulthood (2000).** Ages about 18-29 in rich societies: identity exploration, instability (moves,
  jobs, partners), self-focus, feeling in-between, and a sense of possibilities. Young people mark adulthood by
  accepting responsibility for themselves, making their own decisions and earning their own living, not by weddings
  or degrees. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emerging_adulthood_and_early_adulthood ; Arnett J.J. (2000) Am
  Psychol 55:469-480.
- **Levinson's seasons (1978, 1996).** Alternating stable and transition periods: early adult transition (17-22),
  entering the adult world (22-28), the age-30 transition (28-33), settling down (33-40), midlife transition (40-45),
  entering middle adulthood (45-50), age-50 transition (50-55), culmination (55-60), late adult transition (60-65), late
  adulthood. Key ideas: "the Dream" (a vision of the life one wants), the mentor, and a midlife reappraisal.
  Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Levinson ; Levinson D.J. (1978) The Seasons of a Man's Life; (1996) The
  Seasons of a Woman's Life.
- **Baltes' selection, optimisation and compensation (SOC; Baltes & Baltes 1990).** As resources shrink with age,
  people do well by **selecting** fewer goals (by choice, or because of losses), **optimising** effort on them, and
  **compensating** for losses with new means. The classic example is the pianist Arthur Rubinstein in old age: he played
  fewer pieces, practised them more, and slowed down before fast passages so they still sounded fast. Lifespan view:
  development is gain and loss at every age; plasticity persists. Reference: Baltes P.B. & Baltes M.M. (1990) in
  Successful Aging: Perspectives from the Behavioral Sciences, Cambridge UP.
- **Van Gennep's rites of passage (1909).** Each rite moves a person through separation, a liminal in-between time,
  and incorporation into a new status. Rites of passage are a human universal (Brown 1991).

### B2. Stage by stage

**child (about 4-11)**
- Tasks (Havighurst, middle childhood): learning the physical skills ordinary games need; a healthy attitude to one's
  own growing body; getting along with age-mates; learning a gender role; reading, writing and counting; concepts for
  everyday life; conscience, morality and a scale of values; some personal independence; attitudes to groups and
  institutions.
- Erikson: initiative vs guilt (to about 5), then industry vs inferiority: competence through making things, school
  skills, being good at something.
- What the stage brings: school, first friends and first falling-out, siblings, chores, pets, play every day, being
  compared, fears (dark, strangers, loud noises are human universals), first loss of a grandparent or pet.
- Rites: **Earth**: starting school, birthdays, losing teeth, first communion or similar, first sleepover, first
  bike. **T**: being named after a kinsperson (Ju/'hoansi namesakes are treated as close kin), weaning when the next
  sibling comes (birth spacing 3-5 years), joining the children's play group, a first small catch or first gathered
  food brought home. **M**: naming day at the shrine, first chores for the household, first harvest festival, first
  village fair; the first sign of a gift is possible but rare (magic world only).
- Unlocks juv: puberty (about 11-13), moving up to secondary school; **T**: first menstruation (Ju/'hoansi average age
  16.5 in the 1960s-70s, earlier in better-fed groups) or a boy's first serious hunts; **M**: being bound as an
  apprentice or put into service (about 10-15).

**juv (about 12-17)**
- Tasks (Havighurst, adolescence): new, more mature relations with age-mates of both sexes; a gender role; accepting
  one's body; emotional independence from parents; preparing for partnership and family life; preparing for work;
  a set of values and ethics; wanting and achieving socially responsible behaviour.
- Erikson: identity vs role confusion; loyalty to chosen people and causes.
- What the stage brings: peer acceptance and rejection (youth SRRS 67), crushes and first dating (51), team and club
  selection (55), exams and failing a grade (56), first job, first drink, first rule-breaking, body changes, big
  arguments with parents, first heartbreak (53), driving.
- Rites: **Earth**: confirmation, bar or bat mitzvah, quinceañera, first job, driver's licence, prom, graduation, the
  18th birthday. **T**: Ju/'hoansi first-kill rites for a boy (small cuts and medicine marks after his first antelope),
  the Eland Dance at a girl's first menstruation, Hadza epeme initiation for men who have killed big game and maitoko for
  girls; learning songs, tracking and plant lore. **M**: apprenticeship (several years, food and lodging but no wage),
  confirmation at church or temple, the spring fair and first dances, tending herds alone, first oath.
- Unlocks ya: leaving school; 18 (or the local age of majority); leaving home; first full-time work or study away.
  **T**: marriage (Ju/'hoansi: men in their twenties, women in their teens; bride service hunting for the in-laws);
  **M**: finishing an apprenticeship and becoming a journeyman, or marrying, or inheriting a holding.

**ya (18-29)**
- Tasks (Havighurst, early adulthood): selecting a partner; learning to live with one; starting a family; raising
  children; managing a home; getting started in work; taking on civic responsibility; finding a congenial social group.
- Erikson: intimacy vs isolation. Arnett: identity exploration, instability, self-focus, in-between, possibilities.
  Levinson: entering the adult world, forming the Dream, finding a mentor, the age-30 reappraisal.
- What the stage brings: the most moves, job changes, and partner changes of any stage; first rent and bills, debt,
  first serious job; marriage and first child (birth rates peak at 25-34); peak of accidents, violent victimisation and
  substance problems.
- Rites: **Earth**: moving out, graduation, first paycheck, engagement and wedding, housewarming, baby shower, the 21st
  and 30th birthdays. **T**: marriage (often arranged by parents, often ending: about half of Ju/'hoansi first marriages
  end in divorce, which is accepted), first child, bride service, joining the trance dance as a healer-in-training
  (nearly half of Ju/'hoansi men and a third of women become healers). **M**: journeyman wander-years, wedding at the
  church door, oath to a lord or guild, first tenancy; in a Tolkien-like setting a coming of age can be late (hobbits
  come of age at 33).
- Unlocks adult: about 30, or earlier through commitments: a first child, a settled partner, a settled trade or career,
  a home of one's own.

**adult (30-44)**
- Tasks: Havighurst's early adult tasks continue (raising children, managing a home, building a career) and the
  middle-age ones begin (adult civic responsibility, a standard of living).
- Erikson: intimacy turning into generativity. Levinson: settling down (33-40), becoming one's own person, then the
  midlife transition (40-45) and reappraisal of the Dream.
- What the stage brings: young children (childcare at 2.3 hours a day for children under 6), career peak building,
  promotions, mortgages, the divorce peak in the first decade of marriage, first parent's illness, little free time
  (leisure is lowest at 35-44: 3.9 hours a day).
- Rites: **Earth**: christenings and children's birthdays, promotion, buying a house, the 40th birthday, school events
  as a parent. **T**: becoming a respected hunter or gatherer, hosting hxaro partners, a child's first kill or first
  menstruation rite as a parent. **M**: becoming a master of a craft (with a masterpiece judged by the guild), taking a
  seat at the village moot, inheriting the holding.
- Unlocks mature: about 45; children reaching their teens or leaving; parents in decline; first health warnings.

**mature (45-64)**
- Tasks (Havighurst, middle age): adult civic and social responsibility; keeping a standard of living; helping teenage
  children become responsible and happy adults; adult leisure; relating to one's partner as a person; accepting the
  body's changes; adjusting to aging parents.
- Erikson: generativity vs stagnation. Levinson: middle adulthood and the age-50 transition, culmination. Baltes: SOC
  begins to matter as energy and time are felt to be limited.
- What the stage brings: children leaving home (SRRS 29), parents' deaths, first own chronic illness, peak earnings and
  peak inheritance (near 60), gray divorce, grandchildren (often in the late 40s or 50s), job loss that is hard to
  recover from (re-employment 57% at 55-64).
- Rites: **Earth**: children's weddings, the 50th birthday, silver wedding, becoming a grandparent, parents' funerals.
  **T**: becoming a senior who settles disputes by talk, a storyteller at the fire, an experienced healer. **M**: guild
  warden or alderman, village elder, pilgrimage, handing over the workshop.
- Unlocks elder: about 65; retirement; widowhood; becoming frail; handing on the household or holding.

**elder (65+)**
- Tasks (Havighurst, later maturity): adjusting to decreasing strength and health; to retirement and reduced income;
  to the death of a spouse; finding an affiliation with one's age group; meeting social and civic obligations;
  settling satisfactory living arrangements.
- Erikson: integrity vs despair: making peace with the life one led. Baltes: select, optimise, compensate.
- What the stage brings: retirement (SRRS 45), the most leisure of any stage (7.4 hours a day at 75+), the most
  housework time, falls, hospital stays, deaths of friends and spouse, grandchildren and great-grandchildren,
  dementia risk, one's own death.
- Rites: **Earth**: retirement party, golden wedding, funerals of friends, writing a will, moving to smaller housing.
  **T**: elders as keepers of stories, kin and land knowledge; Ju/'hoansi elders are valued as storytellers at night
  (night talk was 81% stories in Wiessner 2014). Modal age of death for foragers who reach adulthood is about 72
  (Gurven & Kaplan 2007). **M**: elder of the council, keeper of the lore, retiring to a cottage on the holding, last
  rites; in a Tolkien-like world the very old may "pass over the sea" or leave on a last journey.
- End: death.

## Part D. Big life events (84: 42 good, 42 bad)

Big events are rarer than everyday moments and change a life's course. Each entry gives:
- **rates**: typical yearly chance in each of the six stages, for a person in modern Earth (mostly US). **[data]** = from a
  cited source (often the Part A4 table); **[est]** = a reasoned estimate, naming what it leans on. Some rates are per
  person "at risk" (for example divorce per married person); this is said where it applies. "0" = does not happen in
  that stage; "-" = once-in-a-life event already used up for most people.
- **raises / lowers**: which context drivers push the rate up or down. Drivers: family (close, supportive family;
  "family strife" for the opposite), ties (friends and wider social ties), money (wealth; "low money" for poverty),
  health (good health; "poor health"), era (period and technology), harsh (harsh environment), unrest (unstable state,
  war, lawlessness), prosper (economy growing), community (strong local community, church, band), stress.
- **once / repeats**: whether it happens at most once in a life or can come again.
- **responses**: 5+ distinct ways to respond, in the same style tags as Part C.
- **T / M**: how the event looks in the forager band (T) and the magic-world village (M), with the rate shift.

Rate background for the other worlds: in forager bands only about 57% of children reached 15 (45-73% by group); from
15 to about 40 adult deaths ran about 1% a year; life expectancy at 45 was about 20 more years; the modal adult age at
death was about 72 (Hadza 76, !Kung 74). Deaths were about 70% illness, 20% violence and accidents, 9% degenerative
disease; among adults 15-59 violence caused about 17% and accidents 13% (Gurven & Kaplan 2007, Population and
Development Review 33:321-365, https://gurven.anth.ucsb.edu/sites/secure.lsit.ucsb.edu.anth.d7_gurven/files/sitefiles/papers/GurvenKaplan2007pdr.pdf).
Pre-modern Europe looked similar at the population level (Sweden 1751-59: life expectancy at birth 34, at 45 a further
20 years; same source). So in T and M, deaths of children, siblings and parents come about 3-10x as often as on modern
Earth, while the age at which old people die is not very different.

### D6. Loss

Most death rates below are **[est]** built from the SSA 2023 period life table (A4): the chance that a given relative
dies is about that relative's own one-year death risk at their age (parents taken as about 30 years older, siblings
and friends about the same age, spouses about the same age, two children per parent). The commonest very hard event in
the WHO World Mental Health surveys was the unexpected death of a loved one: 31.4% lifetime (Benjet et al. 2016, A3).

- **D51 death of a parent** · bad · loss, family · repeats (twice at most; step-parents add)
  - rates: child 0.4% · juv 0.7% · ya 1.4% · adult 3.5% · mature 8% (per living parent) · elder 15% (the few with a living parent) [data+est: 1 in 11 US children lose a parent or sibling by 18 (Judi's House CBEM, A4); Black Americans are about 3x likelier to lose a mother by 10 (Umberson et al. 2017, PNAS); later stages from the life table]

  - rates (per parent): ya 0.4% · adult 0.5% · mature 0.3% · elder 0.1% [est: leans on "child with a serious illness" at 7.9% lifetime in the WHO World Mental Health surveys (Benjet et al. 2016, A3)]

### D9. Danger, crime and disaster

- **D74 a victim of violent crime (assault, robbery)** · bad · community, body and health · repeats

- **D75 burglary or theft** · bad · home, money · repeats
  - rates (per household): about 10% a year for any property crime, 1.3% for burglary, in every stage; urban 19% vs rural 6% [data: 102 property crimes per 1,000 households a year: burglary 13.1, vehicle theft 6.1, other theft 83.1 (BJS 2023, A4)]

  - responses: [cautious] fit locks and an alarm; [defiant] stake out the house to catch them; [traditional] call the police and the insurance; [kind] help the neighbours who were robbed too; [selfish] claim more on the insurance than was taken; [withdrawn] never feel safe at home again
  - T: rare: few goods and constant sharing; taking without asking is scolded, and hoarding is the worse offence · M: a thief in the henhouse, a cutpurse at the fair, a stolen horse (a hanging matter)
- **D76 arrested, charged or jailed** · bad · community, conflict · repeats
  - rates (arrest): child 0.2% · juv 4% · ya 5% · adult 2.5% · mature 1% · elder 0.2% [data+est: 25-27% of US youth are arrested by 18 and 30-41% by 23, excluding minor traffic offences (Brame, Turner, Paternoster & Bushway 2012, Pediatrics 129:21-27); if 2001 rates held, 6.6% of people born then would go to prison in their lives (Bonczar 2003, BJS)]
  - raises: youth, male, low money, peers who offend, addiction, family strife, city, unrest, harsh policing (era) · lowers: family, school, work, marriage, community, money
  - responses: [cautious] say nothing until a lawyer comes; [defiant] fight the charge all the way; [traditional] confess and take the punishment; [selfish] give up a friend to get a lighter sentence; [kind] protect someone else by taking the blame; [curious] study the law in your cell; [withdrawn] give up on a straight life
  - T: none (no police); public criticism, ridicule, ostracism, and in rare repeated violence, killing by agreement of the band (Boehm 1999) · M: the stocks, the manor court fine, the lord's dungeon, the assize; benefit of clergy for those who can read
- **D77 a house fire** · bad · home · repeats

  - responses: [cautious] fit alarms and plan escape routes afterward; [passionate] run back in for the photographs; [kind] take in the neighbours who lost everything; [traditional] rebuild on the same spot; [selfish] blame the neighbour's barbecue; [withdrawn] can't sleep without checking the stove

- **D78 a natural disaster (flood, storm, earthquake, drought, wildfire)** · bad · home, community · repeats
  - rates: about 2% of the world's people a year (167 million affected in 2024); about 1% a year in the US [data+est: 393 disasters, 16,753 deaths, 167 million affected and about US$242 billion in losses in 2024 (EM-DAT / CRED "2024 Disasters in numbers", https://preventionweb.net/publication/2024-disasters-numbers); US est]

  - responses: [cautious] evacuate early with the go-bag; [defiant] stay and defend the house; [kind] rescue neighbours and run the shelter; [traditional] pray and rebuild exactly as before; [selfish] loot or hoard supplies; [curious] study how to rebuild safer; [withdrawn] move far away and never return
  - T: drought is the great hazard; the Ju/'hoansi answer is to move to kin with water (hxaro ties are insurance); floods and fires in the bush · M: the flood that takes the mill, the hail that flattens the harvest, the hard winter, the landslip in the hills
- **D79 war comes** · bad · community, loss · repeats
  - rates: in a stable rich state under 0.1% a year; worldwide 61 state-based conflicts were active in 36 countries in 2024, the most since 1946, so roughly 1 in 5 countries had a war going on [data+est: PRIO Conflict Trends 1946-2024 (https://www.prio.org/publications/14453); person-level rates est]; young men face combat most in ya
  - raises: unrest, harsh (scarcity), era, low prosper, borders with enemies · lowers: prosper, strong state, peace treaties, community

- **D81 an epidemic sweeps through** · bad · body and health, community · repeats

  - T: small bands limit epidemics, but new diseases from outsiders are deadly; respiratory illness was the commonest cause of forager illness deaths (Gurven & Kaplan 2007) · M: the plague: the Black Death of 1347-51 killed perhaps a third to a half of Europe, and returned every 10-20 years for centuries; smallpox, the sweating sickness

- **D83 a public humiliation or scandal** · bad · community, conflict · repeats
  - rates: child 5% · juv 8% · ya 4% · adult 2% · mature 2% · elder 1% [est: leans on the youth SRRS weight for peer rejection (67 LCU) and the high share of gossip in everyday talk (about a third of Ju/'hoansi day talk was complaint, criticism and gossip, Wiessner 2014)]
  - raises: era (social media), community (everyone knows), conflict, risky behaviour, public roles · lowers: low profile, ties (friends who defend you), moving away
  - responses: [defiant] face it down in public; [withdrawn] leave town or go offline; [traditional] make a public apology and penance; [cautious] say nothing until it blows over; [selfish] blame someone else; [kind] apologise to those you hurt in person; [curious] learn exactly how the story spread
  - T: ridicule is the band's main control; a person who boasts or hoards is mocked until they change or leave (Boehm 1999; Lee 1969) · M: the stocks on market day, "rough music" outside your door, a public penance in a white sheet
- **D84 bullied or cast out by peers** · bad · friends, conflict · repeats
  - rates: child 20% · juv 19% (plus 16% bullied online) · ya 6% (often at work) · adult 5% · mature 4% · elder 3% [data+est: 19% of US high school students were bullied at school and 16% electronically in 2023 (CDC YRBS 2013-2023 Data Summary and Trends, https://cdc.gov/yrbs/dstr/pdf/YRBS-2023-Data-Summary-Trend-Report.pdf); other stages est]

### D10. Using the rates

- Sum of the rates per stage gives roughly how many big events a year a person meets: about 1-2 a year in juv and ya
  (many small ones: breakups, new jobs, moves, fights), fewer in adult and mature, and more losses (deaths, illness,
  falls) in elder. The spec's "slightly more often than in reality" fits a multiplier of about 1.2-1.5 (A4).
- Trouble clusters: prior violence, addiction, low money and family strife raise many bad events at once (Benjet et
  al. 2016, A3); marriage, money and ties lower many at once. The drivers above should therefore move groups of events
  together.
- The list holds 42 good and 42 bad events, and the commonest good ones (new friends, new jobs, falling in love, moves)
  have rates as high as the commonest bad ones; the bad ones carry more weight on the SRRS, so Chroma can keep the
  count balanced while letting losses weigh more.

## Part E. Outside drivers (what the world pushes a person toward)

Frame: Bronfenbrenner's ecological model puts the person inside nested systems: the microsystem (family, friends,
school, work), the links between them, the exosystem (a parent's employer, local government), the macrosystem
(culture, law, economy) and the chronosystem (era and life timing) (Bronfenbrenner U. 1979, The Ecology of Human
Development, Harvard UP). Elder's life-course studies add that the same outside shock (the Great Depression, a war)
hits differently by the age at which it strikes (Elder G.H. 1974, Children of the Great Depression). Below, each
driver says what it **pushes toward** and lists **moments** it creates (Part C ids, Part D ids, or new moment ideas).
World tags: [E] modern Earth, [T] forager band, [M] magic-world village. The person's own filter decides how a push
lands (Part A1, Life Experiences Survey).

Key sources used in this part: peers raise risk-taking in adolescents more than in adults (Gardner M. & Steinberg L.
2005, Developmental Psychology 41:625-635: in a driving game, peers present doubled risky choices in teens, raised them
by half in young adults and not at all in adults); habits and moods spread through networks up to about three steps
(Christakis N.A. & Fowler J.H. 2007, NEJM 357:370-379 on obesity; Fowler & Christakis 2008, BMJ 337:a2338 on
happiness); economic pressure works through parents' stress to harsher parenting (Conger R.D. & Elder G.H. 1994,
Families in Troubled Times); low job control predicts heart disease (Karasek R.A. 1979, Administrative Science
Quarterly 24:285-308; Marmot M.G. et al. 1991, Lancet 337:1387-1393, Whitehall II); teachers' expectations shape pupils
(Rosenthal R. & Jacobson L. 1968, Pygmalion in the Classroom; effect later found smaller than first claimed); mortality
falls in recessions while despair deaths follow long decline (Ruhm C.J. 2000, Quarterly Journal of Economics
115:617-650; Case A. & Deaton A. 2015, PNAS 112:15078-15083); neighbourhoods shape children's later earnings (Chetty R.,
Hendren N. & Katz L. 2016, American Economic Review 106:855-902, Moving to Opportunity); heat raises aggression
(Anderson C.A. 2001, Current Directions in Psychological Science 10:33-38); publicised suicides are followed by more
(Phillips D.P. 1974, American Sociological Review 39:340-354) and stories of coping are followed by fewer
(Niederkrotenthaler T. et al. 2010, British Journal of Psychiatry 197:234-243); religious attendance goes with more
volunteering and giving (Putnam R.D. & Campbell D.E. 2010, American Grace); TV and the decline of clubs and civic
life (Putnam R.D. 2000, Bowling Alone); unilateral divorce laws raised divorce rates for about a decade (Wolfers J.
2006, American Economic Review 96:1802-1820); compulsory schooling laws raised schooling and earnings (Angrist J.D. &
Krueger A.B. 1991, Quarterly Journal of Economics 106:979-1014); a public smoking ban cut heart attack admissions
(Sargent R.P., Shepard R.M. & Glantz S.A. 2004, BMJ 328:977-980).

### F2. What raises and lowers wrongdoing

- **Raises**: youth (peak 15-24), male sex for violence and theft (a human universal, Brown 1991), peers who offend
  (Gardner & Steinberg 2005), drinking, low money, family strife, unrest and weak states, harsh scarcity (theft of
  food), stress, opportunity (unguarded goods, anonymity in cities), prior victimisation (trouble clusters).
- **Lowers**: marriage and children, steady work, community (being known), faith, ties, money, aging. Life-course
  studies find marriage and a good job turn many young offenders away from crime ("turning points": Sampson R.J. &
  Laub J.H. 1993, Crime in the Making, Harvard UP).

### F3. Wrongs in the other worlds

- **T (forager band)**: lying and stinginess are policed by gossip and mockery; theft is rare because there is little
  to take and much is shared, but hoarding and refusing to share are the chief wrongs. No alcohol. Affairs are common
  and the main cause of fights; fights can be deadly because hunting poisons are at hand, so people separate quickly.
  The !Kung homicide rate in 1920-55 was about 42 per 100,000 a year, several times the modern US rate, mostly from
  fights between men (Lee 1979, via https://quillette.com/2017/12/16/romanticizing-hunter-gatherer/). A man who keeps
  bullying or killing may be killed by agreement of the band (Boehm 1993, 1999).
- **M (pre-modern village)**: homicide in medieval Europe ran about 20-40 per 100,000 a year (range 6-150), falling to
  0.7-1.4 by the mid-20th century; killers were mostly young men aged 26-31 fighting acquaintances over honour and
  property, often in taverns and on feast days, with strong drink and knives at hand (Eisner M. 2003, Crime and Justice
  30:83-142, https://www.vrc.crim.cam.ac.uk/system/files/documents/manuel-eisner-historical-trends-in-violence.pdf).
  The manor court fined brawling, short measures of ale and bread, trespass, gleaning out of turn and stray animals;
  theft of a horse or sheep could hang a man; adultery and slander went to church courts; poaching was common and
  harshly punished. In the magic world add oath-breaking, forbidden spellcraft and dealings with dark powers.

## Part G. World notes

### G1. Forager band life (base for the T world, about 40,000 years ago)

Caution: living foragers are not fossils of the past; they have traded, herded and met states for centuries, and
40,000 years ago climates, game and band sizes differed (Ice Age Europe was cold steppe with large game). They are
still the best evidence for how people lived without farms or states. Art and ornament were already present then: hand
stencils in Sulawesi caves date to at least about 45,000 years ago and the Chauvet cave paintings to about 36,000 years
ago; ostrich-eggshell beads were made and traded across southern and eastern Africa for about 50,000 years (Miller J.M.
& Wang Y.V. 2022, Nature 601:234-239).

**Ju/'hoansi (!Kung) of the Kalahari (Lee).** Richard Lee's fieldwork in the Dobe area (1963 onward) found adults spent
about 12-19 hours a week getting food (about 2.4 days of work), or about 40-44 hours with food processing, tool making
and housework counted (Lee R.B. 1968, "What hunters do for a living", in Lee & DeVore eds., Man the Hunter; Lee 1979,
The !Kung San: Men, Women, and Work in a Foraging Society, Cambridge UP; figures via
https://quillette.com/2017/12/16/romanticizing-hunter-gatherer/). Plant foods, above all mongongo nuts, gave most of
the diet; meat was prized and shared by fixed rules, with the arrow's owner (not the shooter) credited. A good hunter
is teased and his meat "insulted" so that no one grows proud (Lee R.B. 1969, "Eating Christmas in the Kalahari",
Natural History 78(10)). Camps of a few related families gathered at permanent waterholes in the dry season and split
up in the rains; people moved between camps often and kept rights to waterhole territories (n!ore) through kin. Names
pass to namesakes, who are treated as close kin. About half of first marriages ended in divorce; a new husband did bride
service in his wife's parents' camp; nearly half of men and a third of women learned to heal in the all-night trance
dance (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C7%83Kung_people). Hard sides: people were thin and often complained of hunger,
and the 1973 drought brought real hunger; infant mortality was about 20%; homicide in 1920-55 ran about 42 per 100,000 a
year, mostly fights between men (same quillette source, citing Lee).

**Hadza of Tanzania (Marlowe).** About 1,200-1,300 Hadza, about a third still living mainly by foraging; camps of
20-30, swelling past 100 in berry season; no real status differences. Men get meat, honey (about 10-20% of calories)
and baobab fruit, usually alone; women dig tubers and gather berries and greens in groups; children forage for part of
their own food from an early age. Marriage is mostly monogamous and serial, couples may live with either spouse's kin,
and camps move for food, for hunting, or to get away from a quarrel. Life expectancy at birth about 33, but adults
often reach their sixties and seventies (Marlowe F.W. 2010, The Hadza: Hunter-Gatherers of Tanzania, U California
Press; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hadza_people). Grandmothers' foraging measurably helps their grandchildren grow
(Hawkes K. et al. 1998, PNAS 95:1336-1339).

**Firelight talk and hxaro (Wiessner).** In 174 Ju/'hoansi conversations recorded in 1974, day talk was 31% economic
matters, 34% complaints, criticism and gossip, 16% jokes and 6% stories; night talk around the fire was 81% stories,
with only 7% complaint and 4% economics. Day talk polices sharing and settles practical matters; night stories build
"imagined communities" with distant kin and allies, teach how people behave, and soothe quarrels (Wiessner P.W. 2014,
"Embers of society: firelight talk among the Ju/'hoansi Bushmen", PNAS 111:14027-14035,
https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2014/09/140922152809.htm). Hxaro is delayed gift exchange (beads, arrows,
ornaments) with partners in many camps; it keeps friendships alive across distance and works as insurance: in a
drought a family goes to live with hxaro partners where there is water and food (Wiessner P. 1982, "Risk, reciprocity
and social influences on !Kung San economics", in Leacock & Lee eds., Politics and History in Band Societies,
Cambridge UP).

**Reverse dominance (Boehm).** Forager equality is not natural ease but work: the group as a whole dominates anyone who
tries to dominate. The steps, from mild to severe, are public opinion and gossip, criticism and ridicule, disobedience,
ostracism or moving away from the offender, and, as a last resort, killing by agreement of the group; the !Kung are
recorded executing extremely aggressive men, and the Hadza use individual lethal retaliation as a levelling threat
(Boehm C. 1993, "Egalitarian behavior and reverse dominance hierarchy", Current Anthropology 34:227-254; Boehm C. 1999,
Hierarchy in the Forest, Harvard UP; summary at https://wiki.p2pfoundation.net/Egalitarian_Behavior_and_Reverse_Dominance_Hierarchy).
Leaders exist only as respected advisers who never give orders.

**Mortality (Gurven & Kaplan 2007).** Across forager groups 57% of children reached 15 (45-73%); of those, about 64%
reached 45; life expectancy at 45 was about 20 more years; the modal adult age at death was about 72 (68-78; Hadza 76,
!Kung 74); adult death rates stayed near 1% a year until about 40 and then doubled every 6-10 years, as in modern
populations. Causes: illness about 70% (respiratory illness the largest share), violence and accidents about 20%,
degenerative disease 9%; among adults 15-59, violence 17% and accidents 13%; among those 60+, degenerative disease 28%.
Over two thirds of those who reach adulthood live to be grandparents, with about 20 years after their last child
(Gurven M. & Kaplan H. 2007, Population and Development Review 33:321-365,
https://gurven.anth.ucsb.edu/sites/secure.lsit.ucsb.edu.anth.d7_gurven/files/sitefiles/papers/GurvenKaplan2007pdr.pdf).

**A forager day, for the engine.** Wake at dawn by the fire; women leave in groups to gather, carrying small children;
men go out alone or in pairs to hunt or check snares, or stay in camp to make arrows and beads; children roam in
mixed-age play groups that teach themselves (Peter Gray, Part C sources); afternoon heat spent in camp talking,
mending, playing, napping; meat shared out on return; evening meal and fire; stories, songs and sometimes an all-night
healing dance. Lean days and feast days alternate with the seasons; quarrels end by talk, teasing or moving camp.

### G2. Pre-modern village life (base for the M world before magic)

**Daily routine.** Work runs by daylight and season. Men plough, sow, mow, reap and thresh; women run the dairy,
brewing, poultry, garden, spinning and children, and join the fieldwork at hay and harvest; children herd, scare birds,
glean and fetch water from about 7. The diet is bread, pottage, ale, cheese and beans, with meat for feasts. Sundays
and holy days stop field work (in principle); how many days peasants actually worked is debated, from about 150 a year
(Schor J. 1991, The Overworked American, after an early estimate by Gregory Clark) to 250-300 (Clark's later view)
(https://www.snopes.com/fact-check/medieval-peasant-only-worked-150-days). Households in north-western Europe were
mostly small (parents and children), people married late (mid-twenties), and many teenagers spent years as servants in
other households before marrying (Hajnal J. 1965, "European marriage patterns in perspective", in Glass & Eversley eds.,
Population in History; Laslett P. 1965, The World We Have Lost). Old people often handed land to a son, a daughter or
even a non-relative under a written maintenance agreement that guaranteed a room, food, fuel and clothing for life
(Clark E. 1982, "Some aspects of social security in medieval England", Journal of Family History 7:307-320).

**The year's festivals (English example).** Twelve days of Christmas; Plough Monday (back to work after Twelfth Night,
the plough blessed or dragged round for money); Candlemas (2 February); Lent; Lady Day (25 March, a rent day); Easter;
Rogation days (processions round the parish bounds to bless the crops); May Day; Whitsun church-ales; Midsummer bonfires
(St John's Eve, 23 June); Lammas (1 August, loaves of the first grain blessed in church); harvest home; Michaelmas
(29 September, rents, accounts and hiring fairs); Martinmas (11 November, slaughter of stock for winter)
(Hutton R. 1996, The Stations of the Sun, Oxford UP; https://www.hanburychurch.org/farming). Feasts bring courting,
drinking and, often, fights.

**Guilds.** A boy is bound apprentice by contract for several years (often seven) for food, lodging and training but no
wage; he becomes a journeyman paid by the day (journée), often travelling; he becomes a master by having a masterpiece
accepted by the guild and paying fees, which lets him open a shop and take apprentices. Guilds set prices, quality and
wages, kept outsiders out, and acted as brotherhoods: feasts, funerals, help for sick members and widows
(https://historycrunch.com/guilds-in-the-middle-ages/).

**Famine.** Harvest failure was the great fear. In the Great Famine of 1315-17 heavy rains ruined grain and hay across
northern Europe; perhaps 10-25% of people died in many towns and 10-15% in southern England; crime surged and
chroniclers recorded desperate acts; full recovery took until about 1325
(https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Famine_of_1315%E2%80%931317; Jordan W.C. 1996, The Great Famine, Princeton UP).
Smaller dearths came every decade or so [est]. Thirty years later the Black Death (1347-51) killed perhaps a third to a
half of Europe and returned for centuries.

**Feuds and violence.** Early medieval law tried to replace blood revenge with compensation (wergild); Icelandic sagas
show feuds settled by arbitration and payment as often as by killing (Miller W.I. 1990, Bloodtaking and Peacemaking,
U Chicago Press). Homicide in medieval Europe ran about 20-40 per 100,000 a year, mostly young men fighting
acquaintances over honour or property, often in taverns or at feasts; it fell ten- to fiftyfold by the 20th century as
states took over justice and public fighting became shameful (Eisner 2003, Part F3). Village shaming ("rough music",
charivari) punished adulterers, scolds and wife-beaters with noise and mockery outside their doors.

**Institutions of the village.** The manor court (fines, land transfers, by-laws for the common fields), the parish
church (baptism, marriage, burial, tithes, holy days), the alehouse (news, deals, quarrels), the guild in towns, the
market and the fair. A Tolkien-like village adds the inn as a hub of news from far away, travellers of other peoples
(elves, dwarves), rangers on the borders, and old tales that turn out to be true.

### G3. Donald Brown's human universals (what every world must have)

Brown listed features found in every known society, ethnographic or historical (Brown D.E. 1991, Human Universals,
McGraw-Hill; list reproduced in Pinker S. 2002, The Blank Slate, appendix; online copy at
https://condor.depaul.edu/mfiddler/hyphen/humunivers.htm). A selection grouped for Chroma, which should therefore
appear in all three worlds:

- **Language and mind**: language, grammar, metaphor, proverbs, personal names, gossip, jokes, insults, lying and
  "language used to misinform", promises, narrative and myths, planning, past-present-future, dreams and their
  interpretation, classification of plants, animals, kin, weather and colours (at least black and white).
- **Society and kin**: family and household, kin terms (mother, father), kin groups, marriage, incest avoidance,
  inheritance rules, age grades and age terms, statuses and roles (some given, some earned), leaders, group living,
  in-group and out-group, ethnocentrism, coalitions, division of labour by sex and age, childcare (with a female focus),
  weaning, socialisation by senior kin, hospitality, visiting, gift giving, reciprocal exchange, trade.
- **Emotion and self**: affection, attachment, crying, empathy, envy, fear (children's fears, fear of death), hope,
  pride, shame, sexual attraction and sexual jealousy, a private inner life, self-image and the wish for a good one,
  facial expressions of anger, disgust, fear, happiness, surprise and contempt.
- **Morality and law**: right and wrong, fairness, admiration of generosity, disapproval of stinginess, rules against
  murder, violence and rape, sanctions (including removal from the group), redress of wrongs, resistance to abuse of
  power, property, taboos, etiquette, self-control.
- **Ritual and belief**: belief in the supernatural, religion, magic (for love, health, plenty), divination, attempts
  to control weather, healing attempts and medicine, death rituals and mourning, rites of passage, feasting, dance,
  music, poetry, body adornment and hairstyles, art.
- **Daily life**: daily routines, meal times, cooking, fire, shelter, tools and tool-making, containers, weapons, toys
  and play (including pretend play), childbirth customs, hygiene, sweet preference, snake wariness, privacy for sex.
- **Conflict**: conflict and its mediation, dominance and submission, prestige and economic inequality, male public
  dominance, male coalitional violence, men's greater proneness to violence, theft and travel over longer distances.

Chroma use: these give a checklist for each world's daily content (every world needs gossip, gifts, feasts, mourning,
rites, healing, jokes, jealousy, quarrels and their mending); differences between worlds are in form and frequency,
not in kind.
